# Family Transition Tool — Quantitative Scale of the Problem

*Research brief supporting the "Family Transition Tool" hackathon project. The emotional hero is the **trailing partner** — the person who relocated for someone else's job, study, or visa and arrives with no natural network. This document assembles defensible hard numbers for the pitch.*

**Geographic labelling:** every figure below is tagged **[National]**, **[Victoria]**, or **[Melbourne]**. Where only a national figure exists, that is stated plainly rather than implied to be local.

**Confidence labelling:** each section flags **Confidence: High / Medium / Low** based on source quality and how directly the source supports the claim. Figures we could **not** independently verify against a primary source are explicitly flagged.

**Date compiled:** 23 May 2026.

## 1. Internal migration churn — how many people move

**Confidence: High.**

- **[National] 14.3%** of the population changed address within Australia in the year before the 2021 Census; 79.3% stayed at the same address. (ABS, *Population movement in Australia*, 2021 Census, published 2022.)
- **[National] 35.3%** of Australians lived at a different address five years before the 2021 Census — broken down as **30.7% who moved within their state (intrastate)** and **3.8% who moved interstate**. (ABS, 2022.)
- **[National]** Over a longer horizon, **more than 40% of Australians change address every five years — about twice the global average.** (ABS, *Population movement in Australia* / Census materials.) *Confidence: Medium — widely cited ABS framing; the directly verified Census figure is the 35.3% over 2016-2021 above.*
- **Trend caveat (for honesty in the pitch):** internal migration has been *declining* over recent decades — the proportion of Australians changing **state** of residence fell by ~20% between 1981 and 2016. (ABS / *The Conversation*, 2020.) The volume is still enormous, but do not claim mobility is rising.

**Why it matters for the tool:** even ignoring international arrivals, roughly **1 in 7 Australians relocates within the country every single year**, and over a third move within any five-year window. Each move can strand a trailing partner.

*Note:* I could not locate a clean, standalone ABS figure isolating *Victoria-specific* annual address-change rates from the same release; the 14.3% / 35.3% figures are national. Flag as national in the pitch.

## 2. International migration & Net Overseas Migration to Victoria/Melbourne

**Confidence: High** (ABS Overseas Migration release).

- **[National]** Net Overseas Migration (NOM) hit a **record ~556,000 in the year ending September 2023**, then **446,000 for the year ending June 2024** — the first annual decrease since border restrictions lifted. The 2022-23 financial-year peak was **536,000**. (ABS, *Overseas Migration, 2023-24*, 2024.)
- **[Victoria]** Victoria recorded a **net gain of 132,860 people through overseas migration in 2023-24.** (ABS, 2024.)
- **[Melbourne]** Melbourne was the **fastest-growing capital city, adding 142,600 people in 2023-24**, the largest net overseas migration gain of any Australian capital. (ABS, *Regional Population, 2023-24*, 2024.) India was the top country of birth contributing to Victoria's net gain.
- **[National] Visa-type composition of arrivals, 2023-24:** International students ~207,000 arrivals; Working Holiday Makers ~80,000; Temporary Skilled ~49,000; Permanent visa holders ~91,000; Visitors ~90,000. (ABS, *Overseas Migration, 2023-24*, 2024.)

**Why it matters:** Victoria is the single largest destination for overseas migration in Australia, and Melbourne is the single largest-growing capital. The trailing-partner problem is concentrated exactly where this tool would launch.

## 3. Family / dependent composition — the "accompanying" partner

**Confidence: High** (Home Affairs Migration Trends; confirmed across multiple secondary citations even though the source PDF blocks automated fetching).

This is the **most pitch-critical section** because it directly sizes the trailing-partner population.

- **[National]** In **2024-25**, of the **132,148 Skill-stream places delivered, only 45.8% went to primary applicants** — meaning **~54% went to secondary applicants (partners and children).** Primary applicants made up **just 32.7% of the total Migration Program.** (Department of Home Affairs, *Australia's Migration Trends 2024-25*, 2025.)
- **[National]** Historical consistency: in 2018-19, primary applicants were **46.2%** of the Skill stream and only **31.6% of the total Migration Program** — the pattern is stable over time. (Home Affairs, *Migration Trends 2018-19*.)
- **[National]** Counting family-stream visas *plus* secondary applicants in other streams, commentators estimate **more than 60% of permanent visas are family-related.** (360info / The Diplomat analysis citing Home Affairs data, 2025.) *Confidence: Medium — this is an analyst aggregation, not a single official line item; use the directly-sourced "~54% of Skill stream are secondary applicants" as the headline.*

**Why it matters — the core insight for the pitch:** For roughly **every skilled migrant who comes for a job, there is more than one accompanying person — a partner or child — who came for someone else.** Secondary applicants are the literal definition of the trailing partner, and they are the *majority* of the skilled intake, not a footnote.

**482 / student dependants:** I could not extract a single clean, current official percentage for the dependant share specifically on subclass 482 (temporary skilled) and student visas within the time available — the relevant Home Affairs detail pages block automated retrieval. The permanent Skill-stream figure (~54% secondary) is the strongest verifiable proxy. Flag the 482/student dependant split as **not independently verified** if a judge presses on it.

## 4. International students in Victoria / Melbourne

**Confidence: Medium** — figures are consistently reported by Study Melbourne and DJSIR (Victorian Government's Department of Jobs, Skills, Industry and Regions), but their pages returned HTTP 403 to automated fetching, so these rest on reported figures rather than a direct primary-source read. Treat the round numbers as government-published estimates.

- **[Victoria]** In **2023, ~235,000 international students** chose to study in Victoria; the sector generated **$14.8 billion in export revenue** and supported **~63,000 jobs.** (DJSIR / Study Melbourne, 2023-24.)
- **[Victoria]** More recent reporting cites **~330,000 international student enrolments in 2024** from over 175 countries, generating **$15.9 billion** and supporting **~64,000 jobs.** (DJSIR / Study Melbourne, 2024.) *Confidence: Low-Medium — the jump from 235k to 330k likely reflects an enrolments-vs-students or counting-method change between sources; do not present both as directly comparable. Lead with the 2023 ~235,000 / $14.8bn pairing, which is the most consistently cited.*

**Why it matters:** International students are a huge slice of recent arrivals (see §2: ~207,000 student arrivals nationally in 2023-24), they are disproportionately concentrated in Melbourne, and they suffer some of the highest loneliness rates of any group (see §5).

## 5. Loneliness & social isolation

**Confidence: High for national general-population figures; Medium for movers/migrant-specific figures** (these lean on academic studies and qualitative research rather than a single national prevalence dataset).

### General Australian population

- **[National] ~1 in 3 Australians feel lonely** some or all of the time — up from **~1 in 4 before COVID** — and **1 in 4 experience persistent loneliness.** Based on a nationally representative survey of 4,026 adults aged 18-92. (Ending Loneliness Together, *State of the Nation: Social Connection in Australia 2023*, 2023.)
- **[National]** Loneliness split by sex: **32% of women and 31% of men.** Those aged **18-24 are the loneliest cohort — about 4× the rate of those 75+.** (Ending Loneliness Together, 2023.)
- **[National]** Scanlon Institute's *Mapping Social Cohesion 2024* found **49% of adults feel isolated from others** often (10%) or sometimes (39%), and **41% feel left out** often or sometimes — consistent since 2021. (Scanlon Foundation Research Institute, 2024.)
- **[National]** Loneliness is estimated to **cost the Australian economy ~$2.7 billion per year**; lonely Australians are **4.6× more likely to have depression.** (Ending Loneliness Together, 2023.)

### Recent movers, migrants and overseas-born

- **[National]** Newly arrived migrants without established networks experience **high loneliness during the initial adjustment period**, compounded by separation from family; visa insecurity prolongs isolation among temporary migrants. (Qualitative academic research, PMC/peer-reviewed, various years.) *Confidence: Medium — strong qualitative consensus but no single clean national prevalence percentage isolating "recent movers" was found.*
- **[National] Women on partner visas** are specifically identified as a high-risk group: the conditional, dependent nature of the partner visa intersects with social isolation to create unique vulnerability (including to coercive control). (PMC peer-reviewed study on migrant precarity, 2024.) *This directly supports the trailing-partner thesis but is qualitative, not a prevalence rate.*

### International students

- **[National] About 2 in 3** international students in an intensive interview study reported **loneliness and/or isolation problems, especially in the early months.** The study also named three distinct forms: *personal* loneliness (loss of family contact), *social* loneliness (loss of networks), and *cultural* loneliness (absence of preferred cultural/linguistic environment). (Sawir, Marginson et al., *Loneliness and International Students: An Australian Study*, Journal of Studies in International Education, 2008.) *Confidence: Medium — influential and widely cited, but 2008 and based on 200 interviews; label the year honestly.*
- **[National]** A 2024 systematic review reported loneliness/isolation as a wellbeing factor in **60-65%** of studies, psychological distress in **31.6-54%**, and that **far fewer international students sought mental-health support than domestic peers (22% vs 57%).** (Systematic review, *Journal of Mental Health* / Taylor & Francis, 2024.)

## 6. Mental-health & settlement difficulty for new arrivals and accompanying partners

**Confidence: Medium** — strongest evidence is academic/longitudinal rather than a single national dashboard.

- **[National]** Increased loneliness during resettlement is associated with **poor general health, PTSD and severe mental illness** among humanitarian migrants — shown in a longitudinal study, establishing a *causal-direction* link between isolation and mental-health deterioration, not just correlation. (Longitudinal study, PubMed, 2019.)
- **[National]** International students show elevated mental-health burden: depression reported in **3.6-38.3%** of studies and anxiety in **2.4-43%** across the literature. (Systematic review, 2024.)
- **[National]** Partner-visa women's dependency on the primary visa-holder is repeatedly identified as a settlement-difficulty and isolation amplifier — the dependent has no independent standing, work history, or network in the new country. (PMC peer-reviewed, 2024.)

**Synthesis for the pitch:** the trailing partner sits at the intersection of three documented risk factors at once — *being a recent mover*, *being a migrant/overseas-born*, and (often) *being a visa-dependent woman*. Each factor independently raises loneliness and mental-health risk; the tool's hero carries all three.

## Honesty / verification notes (read before facing a skeptical judge)

1. **Victoria-specific internal-migration churn rate** — not isolated; the 14.3%/35.3% figures are **national**.
2. **482 and student *dependant* percentages** — not independently verified from a clean official line item (source pages blocked automated retrieval). Use the verified permanent **Skill-stream ~54% secondary** figure as the proxy.
3. **Study Melbourne / DJSIR figures (§4)** — reported, not directly read from the primary page (HTTP 403). The 2023 figure (~235,000 / $14.8bn) is the most consistently cited; the 2024 ~330,000 enrolments figure may use a different counting method — do not present the two as a clean year-on-year comparison.
4. **"More than 60% of permanent visas are family-related"** — an analyst aggregation, not a single official statistic. Lead with the directly-sourced Skill-stream number instead.
5. **Sawir loneliness study (§5)** — 2008, n=200 interviews. Still the canonical Australian international-student loneliness figure, but label the year.
6. **General loneliness "1 in 3 / 1 in 4"** — well-supported by a 4,026-person nationally representative survey; this is among the safest numbers in the deck.
